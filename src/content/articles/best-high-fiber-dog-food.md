High-fiber dog food is food with noticeably more fiber than a standard maintenance diet: on the labels we checked, roughly 10–15% crude fiber on a dry-matter basis, versus a few percent in a typical adult kibble. For anal gland problems, the best high-fiber dog food is one your vet agrees fits your dog, because fiber can help prevent recurrence in dogs with healthy glands but does nothing for an infected or impacted gland.

This guide explains what fiber does, why the crude-fiber number on the bag is misleading, and compares real products on their manufacturer-published numbers. It is research-based, not tested: we have not fed these foods to dogs. If fiber is one piece of a broader plan for an aging dog, start with our [senior dog nutrition guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/senior-dog-nutrition-guide/).

## Key takeaways

- The crude-fiber figure on a label is a maximum that captures mostly insoluble fiber and no soluble fiber. Treat it as a rough floor, not total fiber.
- Anal gland support is the most common reason owners search this topic, but the evidence is expert opinion, not trials (see the dedicated section below).
- Never compare a wet food's fiber percentage with a dry food's as printed. Convert to dry matter first.
- Fiber dilutes calories. It suits overweight dogs and is the wrong call for thin dogs, growing puppies, and dogs with anal sac masses.
- Foods worth comparing: Royal Canin Gastrointestinal High Fiber, Hill's Prescription Diet w/d, Hill's Science Diet Perfect Weight, Nutro Natural Choice Healthy Weight, Natural Balance Fat Dogs.

### What crude fiber does and doesn't measure

Crude fiber is a lab test. It captures most insoluble fiber (cellulose) and essentially no soluble fiber, and on a pet food label it is a maximum, so the real amount in the bag may be lower. Tufts' veterinary nutritionists [explain this](https://sites.tufts.edu/petfoodology/2019/11/04/fiber-frustrations/) and call it a poor measure of actual fiber. Total dietary fiber measures both types but is rarely printed on pet food. So two foods with the same crude-fiber number can behave differently, and a food rich in beet pulp or psyllium (partly soluble) will look lower than it acts. The table is a fair way to rank foods, not a prediction of how your dog's stool will change.

## What does fiber actually do in a dog's body?

Fiber is the part of plant ingredients a dog's own enzymes can't digest. Insoluble fibers (cellulose, wheat bran) add bulk and keep things moving. Soluble fibers (pectins, gums, inulin) dissolve, slow digestion and pull water into the gut. The Tufts guide adds a second dimension, fermentability: how readily gut bacteria break a fiber down into short-chain fatty acids that nourish the colon lining. Highly fermentable fiber can cause gas. Many common sources, including beet pulp, psyllium and oat bran, are mixtures of both types.

## High fiber for anal glands and scooting

This is the page's most-searched question ("best high fiber dog food for anal gland problems", "best dog food to prevent scooting"), so here is the honest version.

**What the glands do.** Dogs have two small anal sacs beside the anus that hold a smelly fluid. Normally a firm stool presses on them and they empty a little with each bowel movement. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center [lists](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/anal-sac-diseases) a low-fiber diet, soft stools or chronic diarrhea, constipation and obesity among the risk factors, and says impaction (sacs that don't empty well) is one of the most common problems and can progress to inflammation, infection or abscess.

**What fiber is supposed to do.** Bulkier, firmer stool presses on the sacs more reliably. Cornell says adding fiber and losing weight "can be beneficial." That is the whole mechanism: fiber helps healthy glands empty on their own. It does not clear a blocked or infected gland.

**How strong is the evidence?** Weaker than the internet implies. A [Dutch survey of veterinarians](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8749694/) (69 dog responses, self-reported) recommends a high-fiber diet for prevention but cites earlier literature for it, did not test diet itself, and states that no clinical trials have evaluated treatment options for anal sac disease. So the recommendation rests on clinical experience and plausible mechanism. Products like Glandex have owner reviews, not trials. A fair summary: fiber is a reasonable, low-risk first step for a dog with healthy glands and soft stools, with no proof it will work for any given dog.

**When scooting means a vet visit.** Scooting is also caused by allergies, worms and other problems. Talk to your vet rather than starting a fiber experiment when you see:

- scooting that lasts more than a day or two, or keeps coming back
- swelling or a bump beside the anus, redness, or bloody or pus-like discharge
- straining, a thin ribbon-like stool, reluctance to sit, a tail held low, or obvious pain
- a strong fishy smell, or constant licking of the area

VCA [advises avoiding stool-bulking diets](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/anal-gland-tumors) (high-fiber included) when an anal sac mass is present, so a dog with a lump should not be put on a high-fiber diet before the vet has seen it. Cornell also notes that vets may express the sacs periodically in recurrent cases.

**A reasonable plan.** Get the vet's all-clear, move to a food from the table over 7 to 10 days, give it a few weeks, and keep a note of stool firmness and scooting. If nothing changes, go back to your vet rather than raising fiber further.

## High-fiber foods for dogs (whole foods and supplements)

If you prefer to boost fiber rather than switch food, these are the usual options. Tufts' table (per tablespoon) shows how different they are:

| Fiber source | Total fiber per tablespoon (Tufts) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Canned pumpkin | 0.4 g | Gentle nudge only; plain purée, not pie filling |
| Wheat bran | 1.6 g (nearly all insoluble) | Bulk-forming; check with your vet first |
| Oat bran | 0.9 g | Mixed soluble and insoluble |
| Psyllium seed husk | 3.5 g (3.0 g soluble) | Most concentrated; needs water |
| Inulin | 2.5 g (all soluble) | Fermentable prebiotic; can cause gas |

- **Pumpkin and green beans.** Tufts calls vegetables like these generally healthy but "variable and typically low" in fiber compared with concentrated supplements. Fine as a cheap first experiment or low-calorie filler. Tufts says to pick pure sources with no added flavors, colors or sweeteners.
- **Psyllium husk.** Strongest per spoonful. VCA's page on psyllium capsules says to always ensure ample fresh water and not to use it in pets suspected to have or at risk of an intestinal blockage. Use a pet-safe product and your vet's guidance on amount.
- **Check labels for xylitol.** Tufts specifically warns to avoid human products containing xylitol or other artificial sweeteners.
- **Introduce slowly,** over several days to weeks, until stool is right or it's clear it isn't helping.

## When might a high-fiber diet help?

- **Anal gland support:** see the section above.
- **Weight management.** Fiber takes up space without adding usable energy, so a dog can eat a satisfying volume on fewer calories.
- **Blood sugar steadiness.** Soluble fiber slows glucose absorption, which is why vets sometimes build it into diabetic plans. Don't adjust a diabetic dog's fiber without your vet.
- **Regularity.** Soluble fiber can draw water in to ease constipation; insoluble fiber absorbs water and firms loose stools. Persistent irregularity is a vet visit, not a food experiment.

## High-fiber puppy food and low-fat high-fiber options

**Puppies.** Growing puppies need nutrient and calorie density, and fiber dilutes both. The [AKC puppy nutrition guide](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/proper-puppy-nutrition-nourishes-rapid-growth/) notes puppies need roughly twice the calories per pound of adult dogs until about 4 to 6 months, and does not list fiber among the essential nutrients. In Puppy Growth Calculator's roundup, a puppy formula (Blue Buffalo Life Protection Puppy) lists only 5% max crude fiber, against 10.5% or more for the weight formulas above. So there is no sensible "high-fiber puppy food" to chase. A puppy with soft stool or scooting needs a vet check, not a weight-loss kibble.

**Low-fat, high-fiber.** Weight formulas pair more fiber with fewer calories, and Natural Balance Fat Dogs lists 7.5% min fat with 10.5% max fiber. Dogs with pancreatitis or other fat-sensitive conditions are a vet decision: do not choose a diet for them from a buying guide.

**Wet food.** High-fiber wet food exists, but compare on dry matter. Hill's w/d canned lists 13.7% fiber on a dry-matter basis. Since canned food is mostly water, its as-fed fiber percentage looks tiny. Wet food can help a dog eat satisfying volume, but the numbers only compare fairly after conversion.

## Soluble vs insoluble fiber: which does what?

For anal gland support, insoluble-heavy fiber is the usual aim because bulk makes bigger, firmer stools. For gut health, fermentable soluble fiber (inulin, FOS, beet pulp) feeds beneficial bacteria. You can't fine-tune this from the label, since the guaranteed analysis gives one crude-fiber number. Read the ingredient list: beet pulp, pumpkin, psyllium, FOS, inulin or chicory root signal functional, partly fermentable fiber; powdered cellulose signals bulk-focused fiber.

## How much fiber is "high fiber"?

There's no regulatory definition. Compared on the labels above, veterinary and weight-management formulas sit around 10–15% crude fiber on a dry-matter basis. K9 of Mine's [roundup](https://www.k9ofmine.com/best-high-fiber-dog-food/) calls roughly 6–10% typical for "high-fiber" foods and says above 10–12% can be problematic. Very high fiber dilutes calories, which is why therapeutic diets belong under veterinary supervision.

## When high fiber is the wrong call

- **Underweight seniors or dogs losing muscle** need calorie-dense food. See our [senior feeding guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-food-senior-dogs/).
- **Dogs with anal sac masses or tumors:** VCA says avoid stool-bulking diets.
- **Puppies and dogs with diagnosed conditions** such as diabetes, kidney disease, pancreatitis or any GI diagnosis: the fiber decision belongs to your vet.
- **Dogs with loose stools from too much food.** Sometimes the fix is portion size, not fiber. See our [less-poop dog food guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-food-for-less-poop/).

## What to look for in a high-fiber food

- **Named, functional fiber sources:** beet pulp, pumpkin, psyllium, brown rice, oatmeal, FOS or chicory root.
- **Protein still first.** A named animal protein should lead the ingredient list.
- **Calories matched to the goal.** For anal gland support in a lean dog, you don't want weight loss as a side effect. Our [calorie calculator](/tools/dog-calorie-calculator/) shows what your dog needs.
- **A transition plan:** over 7 to 10 days, 25% new food, then 50/50, then 75/25. Ease back a step if stools loosen.

## A senior-dog note

A slightly higher-fiber food can help the older dog gaining weight as activity drops. A senior losing weight or muscle needs calorie-dense food. Dogs with underactive thyroids gain weight easily, so check with your vet before layering fiber onto a thyroid plan (see our [hypothyroidism feeding guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-foods-for-hypothyroidism/)). ## FAQ

### What is the best high-fiber dog food?

It depends on the goal. For weight loss, Hill's Science Diet Perfect Weight, Nutro Healthy Weight and Natural Balance Fat Dogs are over-the-counter options with roughly 10–12% fiber on a dry-matter basis. Hill's w/d and Royal Canin Gastrointestinal High Fiber are prescription diets for your vet to recommend.

### What is the best high-fiber dog food for anal gland problems?

There is no proven best food. Vets often recommend more fiber to prevent recurrence in dogs with healthy glands, but the evidence is clinical opinion, not trials. See a vet first for swelling, discharge, pain or persistent scooting.

### Will high-fiber food help my dog's anal glands?

It can support emptying through firmer, bulkier stools. It won't treat an infected or impacted gland, and VCA says to avoid stool-bulking diets if an anal sac mass is present.

### What is a good source of fiber for dogs?

Pumpkin, beet pulp, psyllium husk, brown rice, oatmeal and green beans. Tufts notes vegetables are low in fiber per spoonful compared with psyllium or inulin.

### Is there a high-fiber puppy food?

Not one we'd recommend choosing for fiber. Puppies need calorie and nutrient density, and a typical puppy formula is around 5% max crude fiber. Ask your vet about soft stool in a puppy.

### What is the best dog food to prevent scooting?

Scooting has several causes (glands, allergies, worms), so food alone may not prevent it. A food that produces consistently firm stools helps if glands are the issue. Our [less-poop guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-food-for-less-poop/) covers stool quality.

### Is wet or dry high-fiber food better?

Neither is better; compare on dry matter. Hill's w/d is 14.4% (dry) and 13.7% (canned) dry-matter fiber. Wet food adds water and volume.

### How do I switch to a higher-fiber food?

Over 7 to 10 days: about 25% new food, then 50/50, then 75/25. If stools loosen or gas spikes, hold at the current ratio. If nothing changes after a month, revisit with your vet.

## The bottom line

Fiber is a precision tool. The right amount firms stools, may help healthy anal glands empty, and aids weight control. The wrong amount dilutes nutrition. Shop by ingredients, compare on dry matter, and match the fiber to the dog: a chunky scooter and a frail senior need opposite answers.
