**The best dog food for a Maltese is a complete-and-balanced small-breed formula with small, easy-to-chew kibble for the dog's life stage, fed in measured portions: most adult Maltese need only about 140–270 calories a day.** For wet versus dry, dry is a reasonable default for many adults, while wet food or a wet topper suits seniors, sore mouths and picky eaters.

A healthy adult Maltese weighs only about four to six pounds, according to [VCA Animal Hospitals' breed profile](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dog-breeds/maltese). That tiny body shapes every feeding decision: the kibble has to fit a toy-sized mouth, the portions are so small that a few extra treats matter, and the teeth need more help than most owners expect. Maltese also tend to live a long time, so many spend years as seniors, which is why this guide sits in our senior nutrition section and connects to our [senior dog nutrition guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/senior-dog-nutrition-guide/).

A note on how this page is written: it is research-based. We have not run feeding trials, and the product notes below come from the manufacturers' stated formulas and general veterinary guidance, not from personal testing.

## Key takeaways

- A Maltese needs a small-breed formula with small, easy-to-pick-up kibble, a "complete and balanced" statement for the right life stage, and portions measured precisely, because there is very little room for error in a five-pound dog.
- Dental disease is the breed's best-documented nutrition-adjacent problem. VCA describes Maltese as prone to periodontal problems. Food helps a little; brushing and professional cleanings do the real work.
- Food cannot reliably cure tear staining. Staining is mostly about how tears drain and how visible they are on white fur, so a food switch is not a fix, and sudden heavy tearing needs a vet.
- Very small puppies can drop their blood sugar dangerously low. Feed them often and treat wobbliness or lethargy as an emergency.
- Seniors need fewer calories but the same quality: softer or smaller pieces for sore teeth, regular weigh-ins, and a vet check for any appetite change.
- Foods worth considering below are small-breed formulas from brands vets know. Discuss any change with your vet, especially if your dog has a health condition.

## What does a Maltese need from its food?

Four traits drive the choice.

**A very small mouth.** Standard adult kibble is sized for dogs many times heavier. Small-breed formulas use smaller, easier-to-chew pieces. If your Maltese pushes kibble around the bowl, drops it, or swallows it whole, the size is probably wrong.

**A tiny daily ration.** Small dogs need very few calories in total, and VCA's breed profile notes that small snacks add up quickly for a dog this size. A handful of treats can be a large share of the day's food.

**Fragile teeth and gums.** The same VCA profile says the breed is prone to periodontal problems and that regular brushing can prevent much of it.

**A small-dog metabolism with a long lifespan.** Calorie density matters for puppies and active adults, while seniors usually need to be watched for weight gain or loss.

For general background on how to compare foods, the [WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines](https://wsava.org/global-guidelines/global-nutrition-guidelines/) hub points owners to its "Selecting a Pet Food" tool and food label guides, and warns that an ingredient list on its own tells you nothing about ingredient quality.

## What to look for on the label

- **A life-stage statement.** Look for wording that the food is "complete and balanced" for growth, adult maintenance, or all life stages, as appropriate. A breed name on the bag is marketing; the nutritional adequacy statement is the part that matters.
- **Small kibble.** Small-breed or toy-breed formulas are designed for small jaws. Check the product description for the piece size and watch how your dog actually eats it.
- **A named manufacturer you can contact.** The WSAVA guidance for owners encourages looking beyond the ingredient list. Companies that employ qualified nutritionists and can answer questions about their foods are a safer bet than ones that cannot.
- **Moderate fat and controlled calories.** Many small dogs gain weight easily, and some are sensitive to rich food. If your Maltese has had pancreatitis or recurring stomach upsets, ask your vet before choosing a rich formula.
- **Dental features.** Some formulas include ingredients meant to reduce tartar. These can help between brushings but do not replace them.
- **Simple, digestible recipes for sensitive stomachs.** If your dog has loose stools or gas, a gentler recipe is worth discussing with your vet. Our [less-poop guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-food-for-less-poop/) covers digestibility in more detail.

## Does the right food stop tear staining?

Honestly, no, not reliably. Reddish-brown staining under a white Maltese's eyes is one of the most searched concerns for the breed, so it deserves a straight answer.

Tear stains come from tears that overflow and sit on the fur. According to [VCA's overview of epiphora](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/eye-discharge-or-epiphora-in-dogs), excess tearing happens when drainage is insufficient or production is too high. Causes range from poor drainage (for example hair or debris blocking the duct) to overproduction from infection, inflammation, allergies, abnormal eyelashes or eye injuries. White fur simply shows the staining more than dark fur does.

What that means for feeding:

- **See the vet first.** Heavy or sudden tearing, discharge, squinting or redness can signal a corneal injury or other problem that needs treatment.
- **Do not trust miracle claims.** Products that promise to clear stains through a food or supplement change often lack good evidence. If a food seems to help, it may be coincidence, or a food sensitivity that was making eyes water.
- **Keep the area clean and dry.** Gently wiping the face with a vet-approved product and trimming hair around the eyes helps with the staining you can see.
- **Consider allergies.** If tearing comes with itching, ear problems or digestive upset, ask your vet about a food trial done properly, not random brand-hopping.

## Feeding a Maltese with a sensitive stomach

Some Maltese have touchy digestion. Signs include loose stools, gas, or an occasional vomit of yellow bile on an empty stomach. Practical steps:

- Switch foods gradually over about a week by mixing the new food in with the old.
- Change one thing at a time, so you know what caused any problem.
- Use small, regular meals rather than one large meal.
- Be strict with treats and table scraps, which are a common cause of upset in small dogs.
- See your vet for vomiting that repeats, bloody stools, or any lethargy or appetite loss. These are not "sensitive stomach" territory.

## Weight control in a very small dog

At four to six pounds, a few extra ounces are proportionally large, and VCA's Maltese profile advises restricting calories if a dog gains too much weight. Weigh regularly on a kitchen or baby scale, measure food instead of scooping, use pea-sized treats, and learn body condition scoring: you should feel the ribs under a thin layer of coat and flesh. If you can't, ask your vet for a target weight and calorie amount.

## Hypoglycemia in tiny dogs

Very small puppies are prone to low blood sugar. VCA's Maltese profile advises feeding them often, with small meals that are high in protein, fat and complex carbohydrates, which "may help guard against" the problem. Never leave a tiny puppy without food for long stretches. Weakness, wobbling, tremors, glassy eyes or collapse in a small dog are emergencies: contact a vet immediately. Some adult toy dogs that skip meals or become unwell can also be at risk, so poor appetite in a very small dog should not be ignored.

## Feeding the senior Maltese

Toy breeds are often considered senior from around 10 years, though it varies, and Maltese commonly live well beyond that. Because "senior" can be a long chapter, the way you feed changes in stages:

- **Calories usually come down, quality stays up.** Metabolism slows, but older dogs still need good protein and balanced nutrition. A senior or "mature adult" small-breed formula is built around that trade-off. Always check the label's life-stage statement.
- **Softer food for sore mouths.** Dental disease catches up with many older Maltese. Soaking kibble in warm water for 10 to 15 minutes, or mixing in a spoonful of a matching wet food, can make meals comfortable. Softer food is kinder on gums but does less to keep teeth clean, so brushing and vet cleanings matter more.
- **Smaller pieces and shallow bowls.** Some seniors with painful teeth or neck stiffness do better with the tiniest kibble and a bowl that is easy to reach.
- **Pickier appetite.** Gently warming food releases aroma and can tempt a senior. But a sustained drop in appetite is a reason to see the vet, not to keep swapping foods.
- **Weigh monthly, and look at the whole picture.** Weight loss, increased thirst, accidents in the house or changes in energy can be early signs of illness, not just aging.
- **Regular vet checks.** Bloodwork and dental exams guide whether your senior needs a special diet, for example for kidney, heart or liver conditions. Do not start a prescription-style diet on your own.

## Dental disease and food

VCA's profile of the breed says Maltese are prone to periodontal problems. The Merck Veterinary Manual's owner page on [disorders of the mouth in dogs](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/digestive-disorders-of-dogs/disorders-of-the-mouth-in-dogs) says gum disease is the most common oral problem in small animals.

In practice: brush daily with dog toothpaste (never human toothpaste), keep professional cleanings on your vet's schedule, and treat chewing on one side, dropping food, bad breath or drooling as a reason to book an exam. Don't rely on kibble to clean teeth; a little crunch may help but is no substitute for brushing.

## How much to feed a Maltese by weight

Estimated calories per day, computed with the resting energy requirement formula **RER = 70 × (weight in kg)^0.75**, times a life-stage factor (1.6 neutered adult, 2.0 puppy 4 months or older), as published by Jennifer Coates, DVM, in [PetMD's calorie guide](https://www.petmd.com/dog/nutrition/how-many-calories-does-a-dog-need). Pounds are divided by 2.2 to get kilograms, and results are rounded to the nearest 5.

| Body weight | RER (resting) | Neutered adult (×1.6) | Puppy 4 months+ (×2.0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 lb (1.4 kg) | 90 kcal | 140 kcal | 175 kcal |
| 5 lb (2.3 kg) | 130 kcal | 205 kcal | 260 kcal |
| 7 lb (3.2 kg) | 165 kcal | 265 kcal | 335 kcal |
| 10 lb (4.5 kg) | 220 kcal | 350 kcal | 435 kcal |

Worked example: a 5 lb neutered adult is 2.27 kg. 2.27^0.75 is about 1.85, times 70 is 129 kcal (RER), times 1.6 is about 207 kcal a day.

**Cups depend on your food.** The bag lists kilocalories per cup, and it varies a lot. Divide the daily calories by that number: a 5 lb adult on a food with 400 kcal per cup needs about half a cup a day (an example, not a recommendation). Our [dog calorie calculator](/tools/dog-calorie-calculator/) does the maths. Intact, very active, underweight and senior dogs differ, and treats count toward the total, so adjust by body condition and your vet's advice. Most adult toy dogs do well on two or three small meals a day, and puppies need more frequent meals.

If your dog is losing weight without trying, gaining quickly, or leaving meals regularly, see your vet before changing the amounts.

## What to feed a Maltese puppy

Choose a growth (puppy or all-life-stages) small-breed formula with the smallest kibble you can find, and feed small meals often. VCA advises frequent small meals for Maltese puppies to guard against hypoglycemia (see above). Moistening kibble with warm water helps tiny mouths, and the puppy column in the table gives an estimate for puppies 4 months and older (younger puppies use a higher factor of 3.0, so ask your vet). Switch foods gradually over about a week, and start tooth-brushing early.

## Wet or dry food for a Maltese?

- **Dry food** is a reasonable default for many adults: convenient, easy to measure, and a little crunch may help.
- **Wet food or a wet topper** suits seniors, dogs with sore teeth and picky eaters, and adds aroma. Count its calories too.
- **Mixing** works well, as long as the total stays inside the calorie target above.
- **If you feed mostly soft food**, step up brushing and dental checkups.

## Foods and ingredients to avoid

A toxic dose is tiny in a five-pound body, so these matter more here:

- **Xylitol.** Found in sugar-free gum, some peanut butters, candies and baked goods. [VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/xylitol-toxicity) calls it extremely toxic to dogs, causing a rapid drop in blood sugar and, at higher doses, liver failure. Call a vet or poison helpline immediately if your dog may have eaten any.
- **Chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions, garlic and macadamia nuts.** All are unsafe for dogs.
- **Fatty table scraps.** Rich human food can upset small stomachs and is risky for dogs prone to pancreatitis.
- **Large or hard treats and bones.** Choking and tooth-fracture risks are higher in a toy mouth.
- **Frequent menu changes.** Constant rotation can upset digestion and encourage picky eating.

## FAQ

### What is the best dog food for a Maltese?

There is no single best food. Choose a complete and balanced small-breed formula with small kibble for your dog's life stage, check how your dog digests it, and confirm with your vet. The options above are a starting shortlist.

### How much should I feed my Maltese?

A neutered 5 lb adult needs roughly 205 calories a day by the standard formula, and a 7 lb dog about 265. Convert that to cups using the kcal per cup on your bag, or use our [calorie calculator](/tools/dog-calorie-calculator/), and adjust by body condition.

### What size kibble does a Maltese need?

Small or toy-breed kibble. If your dog struggles to pick it up, drops it or gulps it whole, try a smaller piece or soften it with warm water.

### Can food get rid of my Maltese's tear stains?

Not reliably. Tear staining is mostly linked to how tears drain and how visible they are on white fur. Have a vet rule out eye problems, keep the face clean, and be cautious about products that promise a food-based cure.

### What is the best dry or wet dog food for a Maltese?

Dry food is a reasonable default for many adults. Wet food or a wet topper can suit seniors, dogs with sore teeth and picky eaters. No single brand wins for every dog: pick a complete-and-balanced small-breed recipe for the life stage and match the portion to the calorie table above. If you feed mostly soft food, pay extra attention to brushing and dental checkups.

### When should I switch my Maltese to senior food?

Many toy breeds are treated as seniors from around 10 years, but go by your dog's condition and your vet's advice rather than a birthday. Switch gradually over about a week.

### What should I feed a Maltese puppy?

A puppy or all-life-stages small-breed food in tiny kibble, fed in small, frequent meals. Wobbling, weakness or tremors in a small puppy are emergencies.

### Why does my Maltese keep skipping meals?

Check for dental pain first, then illness. Skipped meals are riskier in a very small dog, so persistent refusal means a prompt vet visit.

## The bottom line

For a Maltese, the basics do most of the work: small kibble, a complete and balanced formula for the right life stage, measured portions, and consistent tooth care. Do not expect food to erase tear stains, and do not ignore appetite changes in a dog this small. As your Maltese ages, soften textures if needed, weigh monthly, and lean on your vet.
