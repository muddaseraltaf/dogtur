To cook beef liver for dogs, simmer plain liver in unsalted water for about 10–15 minutes until it is grey-brown all the way through with no pink, let it cool, then cut it into pea-sized pieces. Add no salt, oil, butter, onion or garlic. Then feed it only as a small treat, because liver is so rich in vitamin A that too much, too often, can harm a dog.

Cooking liver is easy; knowing how much to give is where most guides fall short. Below: boiled, baked and dried methods, a per-size amount table we calculated from published numbers, and the dogs who should skip liver. This is research-based, not tested in our kitchen, and not a substitute for your vet. For the whole diet, see our [senior dog nutrition guide](/senior-dog-nutrition/senior-dog-nutrition-guide/).

## Key takeaways

- Simmer plain liver 10–15 minutes until no pink remains, cool, cut small. No seasoning.
- Liver is a treat, not a meal: Merck Veterinary Manual says feeding only liver can induce vitamin A toxicity in dogs.
- Cooked is safer than raw (FDA and CDC warn about Salmonella and Listeria).
- For a 20 lb adult dog the 10% treat cap is about 31 g of cooked liver a day on calories alone, but vitamin A is the real limit, so give far less.
- Ask your vet first if your dog has liver disease, a copper storage risk, pancreatitis history, or is a puppy or pregnant.

## How do you cook beef liver for dogs?

Every method follows the same three rules: nothing added, cooked through, cut small.

| Method | Time | Result | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiled / simmered | 10–15 min | Soft, moist cubes | Training treats, mixing into food, senior dogs with sore teeth | Rinse off the grey foam; save the broth only if it is unsalted |
| Baked | 15–20 min at 175 °C / 350 °F | Firmer, drier pieces | Slightly chewier treats | Overcooking makes it hard and crumbly |
| Dehydrated / dried | 6–10 hours (depends on slice thickness) | Hard, shelf-lighter jerky | Long storage, high-value training rewards | Calories per gram rise sharply once water is gone; weigh before you dry |

Times are practical kitchen ranges, not a lab standard. Judge by the inside of the piece, not the clock.

### How to boil liver for dogs (the most common method)



1. **Rinse and trim.** Rinse the liver under cold water. Cut away visible tubes and any tough membrane. Cut the liver into 2–3 cm (about 1 inch) slices so it cooks evenly.
2. **Simmer, don't rapid-boil.** Put the slices in a pot, cover with plain water (no salt, no stock cubes, no onion), bring it to a gentle boil, then lower to a simmer.
3. **Cook 10–15 minutes.** Thicker slices need the longer end. Cut one slice open: it should be uniformly grey-brown with no pink or red.
4. **Cool completely.** Drain and let the liver cool on a plate. Hot liver is easy to over-feed and can burn a dog's mouth.
5. **Cut small.** Cut into pea-sized pieces for small dogs or fingernail-sized pieces for large dogs. A little goes a long way (see the amount table below).

The FDA says home-cooked pet food should reach a safe internal temperature checked with a food thermometer, and that thorough cooking kills Salmonella, Listeria and other bacteria. It gives no number for liver. The usual human food-safety figure for organ meat is 160 °F (71 °C), which we could not confirm on the USDA chart, so treat it as a kitchen convention.

### How to bake liver for dogs

1. Heat the oven to 175 °C (350 °F).
2. Lay 5–8 mm (about 1/4 inch) slices on a baking tray lined with baking paper. No oil, no butter, no seasoning.
3. Bake about 15–20 minutes, turning once, until firm and dry-looking with no pink in the centre.
4. Cool fully, then cut small.

### How to make dried (dehydrated) liver treats

1. Boil or bake the liver first. Do not skip this. The CDC notes that freeze-drying, dehydrating or freezing only reduces germs in raw products rather than eliminating them, so drying raw liver is not a safe shortcut.
2. Slice thin (about 5 mm) and spread on dehydrator trays or an oven rack at the lowest setting.
3. Dry until hard and snap-dry, usually several hours. A thin slice dries faster than a thick one.
4. Cool, then store airtight.

Dried liver is the easiest to overdo: a 10 g dried piece represents far more liver, vitamin A and calories than 10 g of boiled liver. Weigh the boiled liver before drying and count it that way.

### How long does cooked liver last?

As a general food-safety habit, keep cooked liver airtight in the fridge for about 3–4 days, or freeze treat-sized portions for a few months. We could not open the USDA storage chart to confirm those numbers, so treat them as rules of thumb and discard anything that smells off or turns soft and damp.

## Is beef liver good for dogs?

Yes, in small amounts. Beef liver is nutrient-dense, which is why it makes a high-value training treat. According to USDA FoodData Central (entry for beef liver, cooked, braised), 100 g contains about 191 kcal, 29 g protein, 5.3 g fat and 9,442 micrograms of vitamin A (as retinol activity equivalents).

That vitamin A number is the whole story. It is excellent in small doses and the reason liver cannot be a daily staple. Per the Merck Veterinary Manual, hypervitaminosis A (vitamin A toxicity) is rare in dogs, and dogs appear less sensitive to excess vitamin A than cats, but "feeding only liver can induce vitamin A toxicity in both dogs and cats." The same manual's section on spinal and bone disorders notes that animals fed diets made largely of liver are at risk of chronic hypervitaminosis A, with signs developing after months of excess intake, and that stopping the excess does not reverse the bone changes already present.

One liver treat is fine; a bowl every day for months is a problem. Signs to discuss with your vet include stiffness, lameness, weight loss, poor appetite and lethargy, though none are specific to vitamin A.

## How much liver can a dog eat?

There is no official number for treat liver, so be wary of anyone quoting one confidently. We cap liver by calories (the 10% treat rule) and explain why vitamin A means you should stay well below that.

A frequently quoted guideline in raw and homemade feeding circles is that organ meat as a whole should be a small share of the diet, often given as about 5% for liver. We could not trace that figure to a primary veterinary source, so treat it as a common guideline, not a verified limit, and ask your vet or a board-certified veterinary nutritionist before using it.

### Original table: how much cooked liver per day by dog size

We computed this from published numbers rather than testing anything.

**How the numbers were calculated**

- Resting energy requirement: RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75
- Daily calories for a neutered adult at a healthy weight: RER × 1.6 (Jennifer Coates, DVM, PetMD)
- Treat cap: 10% of daily calories (the standard treat budget)
- Liver weight: treat calories ÷ 1.91 kcal per gram (USDA FoodData Central, beef liver, cooked, braised: 191 kcal per 100 g)

| Dog weight | Daily calories (neutered adult) | 10% treat budget | Maximum cooked liver if liver were the only treat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 lb (2.3 kg) | ~207 kcal | ~21 kcal | ~11 g (0.4 oz) |
| 10 lb (4.5 kg) | ~348 kcal | ~35 kcal | ~18 g (0.6 oz) |
| 20 lb (9.1 kg) | ~585 kcal | ~59 kcal | ~31 g (1.1 oz) |
| 30 lb (13.6 kg) | ~794 kcal | ~79 kcal | ~42 g (1.5 oz) |
| 50 lb (22.7 kg) | ~1,164 kcal | ~116 kcal | ~61 g (2.1 oz) |
| 70 lb (31.8 kg) | ~1,498 kcal | ~150 kcal | ~78 g (2.8 oz) |
| 90 lb (40.8 kg) | ~1,809 kcal | ~181 kcal | ~95 g (3.3 oz) |

**Read this table as a ceiling, not a recommendation.**

- It treats the whole 10% treat budget as liver, leaving nothing for other treats, chews or food used in training.
- Calories are not the real limit. Vitamin A is. For scale, 31 g of cooked liver (the 20 lb row) carries roughly 2,900 micrograms of vitamin A (RAE), all of it preformed, in one day. We have not found a vet-approved daily safe limit for treat liver in dogs, so we are not going to invent one. The calorie ceiling is simply the largest amount this method can justify.
- Our own conservative suggestion (not a veterinary standard): a few small pieces a couple of times a week, not every day.
- Calculate overweight dogs at target weight; seniors who eat less have a smaller cap. For your dog's exact number use our free [dog calorie calculator](/tools/dog-calorie-calculator/) and take 10%.


Ask your vet before making liver regular, especially if your dog's food already contains liver or organ meat.

## Raw vs cooked liver: which is safer?

Cooked. Raw liver can carry bacteria, and the people in your house handle it too.

- The FDA says raw pet food is more likely than other pet foods to carry disease-causing bacteria: in its 2010–2012 study, 15 of 196 raw samples tested positive for Salmonella and 32 for Listeria monocytogenes. It also says owners who feed raw food may face a higher risk of infection, because bacteria can spread from the food to hands and surfaces.
- The CDC does not recommend feeding raw pet food or treats to dogs or cats, and notes that freezing, freeze-drying or dehydrating only reduces germs, it does not eliminate them.


Some owners feed raw liver as part of a raw diet. Talk to your vet first. For a plain treat, cooked is the safer default.

## Beef liver vs chicken liver vs lamb liver

| Liver | Typical use | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|
| Beef | Easy to buy, large pieces to slice | Very high in vitamin A (about 9,442 µg RAE per 100 g cooked, braised, per USDA) |
| Chicken | Small, cheap, cooks quickly | Also vitamin A rich; smaller pieces make it easier to portion treats |
| Lamb | Alternative for dogs that tire of beef | Also vitamin A rich; same rules apply |

We verified the USDA figure only for beef. Do not assume chicken or lamb liver is "safer": all liver is concentrated organ meat and the same small-treat rule applies. Beef-allergic dogs should not get beef liver; see our guide to [dog food for allergies and yeast infections](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-food-for-allergies-and-yeast-infection/).

## Which dogs should avoid or limit liver?

Ask your vet before feeding liver at all if your dog fits any of these.

- **Liver disease or copper storage disease risk.** VCA says copper buildup in the liver can come from a defect in copper metabolism or too much dietary copper, names Bedlington Terriers, Dobermans and Labrador Retrievers as breeds with a recognized copper-regulation problem, and says a low-copper diet may be recommended. PetMD lists liver among high-copper foods to avoid for affected dogs. Not every Lab has the condition, but ask your vet.
- **Dogs prone to pancreatitis.** Cooked liver is fairly lean (about 5 g of fat per 100 g in the USDA entry), but anything you cook it in, such as oil or butter, adds fat. If your dog has a pancreatitis history, ask your vet about every treat.
- **Puppies and pregnant dogs.** We found no source for a safe amount, so ask the vet.
- **Dogs on a prescription diet.** Treats can undermine a therapeutic diet, so check first.

## Never add onion, garlic or salt

Plain means plain. The ASPCA says onions and garlic can irritate the stomach and damage red blood cells, which may lead to anemia, and that excess salt can cause increased thirst and urination and, in severe cases, tremors and seizures. That is why "liver and onions", seasoned stock and the leftover pan from your own dinner are all off the menu. The same goes for butter, oil and spice blends.

## How to feed liver treats

Count them inside the 10% treat budget (use the [dog calorie calculator](/tools/dog-calorie-calculator/)), use a pea-sized piece as a reward, and introduce slowly, since a rich new food can loosen stools. If your senior's stools are already a concern, see our guides to [high-fibre dog food](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-high-fiber-dog-food/) and [dog food for less poop](/senior-dog-nutrition/best-dog-food-for-less-poop/). Stop and call your vet if your dog vomits, has lasting diarrhoea, or seems stiff or lethargic.

## FAQ

### How do you cook beef liver for dogs?

Simmer plain beef liver in unsalted water for about 10–15 minutes until it is grey-brown through with no pink, cool it, and cut it into small pieces. You can also bake it at about 175 °C (350 °F) for 15–20 minutes or dehydrate it after cooking. Never add salt, oil, butter, onion or garlic.

### How long should I boil liver for dogs?

Most 2–3 cm slices need about 10–15 minutes at a gentle simmer. Cut a piece open to check: it should be evenly cooked with no pink. Thick pieces take longer, thin pieces less. The FDA's guidance on cooking homemade pet food is to use a food thermometer to check a safe internal temperature.

### Can dogs eat cooked beef liver?

Yes, plain cooked beef liver is fine for most healthy dogs in small amounts. The limit is vitamin A: Merck Veterinary Manual says feeding only liver can induce vitamin A toxicity in dogs. Treat liver as an occasional reward, count it in the 10% treat budget, and ask your vet if your dog has liver disease or a copper storage disease risk.

### Is boiled liver good for dogs?

Yes. Boiled liver is soft, easy to cut and needs no added fat. It is still a concentrated source of vitamin A, so the amount matters more than the method.

### How much liver can I give my dog?

On calories alone, the 10% treat cap works out to roughly 11 g for a 5 lb dog, 31 g for a 20 lb dog and 61 g for a 50 lb dog of cooked liver per day if liver were the only treat. Vitamin A is the real limit, and we found no vet-approved daily number, so use less than the cap, give it a few times a week rather than daily, and check with your vet.

### Can I feed my dog raw beef liver?

It is safer to cook it. The FDA found Salmonella and Listeria in raw pet food samples and warns that raw feeding can raise infection risk for owners too, and the CDC does not recommend feeding raw pet food or treats. Ask your vet if you are considering a raw diet.

### Can dogs eat liver every day?

We would not make it a daily habit. Merck Veterinary Manual describes chronic vitamin A toxicity in dogs and cats fed diets made largely of liver, with signs developing after months. Ask your vet what is safe for your dog.

### Which dogs should not eat liver?

Dogs with liver disease or copper storage disease, breeds such as Bedlington Terriers, Labrador Retrievers and Dobermans that are predisposed to copper problems, dogs with a pancreatitis history, puppies, pregnant dogs and dogs on prescription diets should only have liver if their vet says so.
