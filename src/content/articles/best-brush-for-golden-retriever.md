The best brush for a Golden Retriever is not one brush but a small kit: a pin brush for daily upkeep, a slicker or undercoat rake for loose undercoat, and a steel comb to check the feathering. Goldens have a thick double coat that sheds a little all year and a lot twice a year, so the right tool and routine matter more than any single brand. This guide explains what each tool does, how often to use it, how to handle shedding season and mats, and how to adjust for an older Golden. We have not tested any of the products below ourselves, and the pick list rests on published grooming guidance and manufacturer listings.

## Key takeaways

- Goldens have a thick, water-repellent double coat. The American Kennel Club (AKC) says it sheds moderately year-round and heavily once or twice a year.
- A Golden Retriever Club of America (GRCA) education director advises a daily pin brush session during heavy shedding and weekly brushing otherwise, plus brushing before and after every bath.
- A slicker brush, undercoat rake, and steel comb each do a different job. Most Goldens do well with a pin brush plus one undercoat tool and a comb.
- Deshedding blades are heavy-duty tools. Use them lightly, only in shedding season, and skip them on thin or sensitive skin.
- Do not shave a Golden. Shaving removes both coat layers, does not cool the dog, and the coat can grow back unevenly.
- Check behind the ears, under the legs, and around the collar for mats. For senior Goldens, use gentler tools, shorter sessions, and treat every brushing as a lump check.

## Why does a Golden Retriever's coat need special brushes?

**A Golden has two coat layers, and a brush has to reach the soft undercoat, not just smooth the top.** The AKC describes a double coat as a harsh outer layer of coarse guard hairs that repels dirt and water over a soft, dense undercoat that insulates. The GRCA's breed standard calls for a coat that is "dense and water repellent with good undercoat," and says the body coat should show no signs of clippering. The Golden's job as a retriever is why the coat matters: the two layers together work like a waterproof jacket.

That structure is why a basic bristle brush disappoints. GRCA education director Nancy Talbott, quoted by the AKC, says bristle brushes "won't get through the thick Golden coat." A brush that only skims the surface leaves dead undercoat trapped against the skin, where it can felt into mats. VCA Hospitals also groups retrievers with medium-length shedding coats and recommends combs or stiff bristle brushes, with long-toothed combs to pull out loose undercoat clumps during seasonal shedding.

## What does each brush type do?

**Each tool covers a different layer and a different job. Pick by job, not by marketing name.**

### Pin brush: the daily tool

The AKC's Golden grooming guide names the pin brush as the main tool for daily brushing during heavy shedding and for back-brushing the whole body to loosen dead undercoat and debris. A pin brush has widely spaced pins with rounded tips, usually on a cushioned pad. It is gentle, which makes it the best everyday choice and the right one for seniors. Its limit is depth: reviewers note that short pins struggle to reach a dense undercoat, so choose longer pins and use the brush against the direction of growth, then smooth it back.

### Slicker brush: for the thick spots

A slicker has fine bent wire pins close together. The AKC's double-coat guide says slickers work well in areas with heavier coat density. On a Golden they help on the thick spots: the hindquarters, the thick "pants," and the ruff. They also pick up tangles. The tradeoff is scratch risk. Slickers can irritate skin if you press, so use light strokes and stop if the skin looks pink. Self-cleaning versions let you retract the pins to release hair, which saves time.

### Undercoat rake: the shedding-season workhorse

An undercoat rake has one or two rows of long, rounded teeth that comb through the topcoat and pull out loose undercoat. The AKC's double-coat guide names a grooming rake or quality steel comb for removing loose undercoat. A rake is often the most effective tool during the heavy shed. Choose a rake with rounded tips and keep it moving; pushing a rake against a mat can pull painfully.

### Deshedding tool: use sparingly

Deshedding tools, such as the FURminator-style blade, use a fine comb edge to pull out large amounts of undercoat quickly. Review sites disagree on whether they are essential or too harsh for everyday use. Our position is cautious: the AKC's Golden grooming guide does not mention deshedding tools at all, and the AKC's double-coat guide lists a rake, comb, and slicker. If you use a blade, go lightly, a few strokes per area, only in shedding weeks, and never over bony areas, irritated skin, or mats. Overuse can irritate the skin.

### Steel comb: the check tool

The AKC's Golden guide recommends a metal comb with medium to wide teeth for the feathering behind the ears, on the chest, on the legs, and on the tail. A comb is not for shedding but for finding tangles the brush skipped. If a comb catches in the feathering, you have found a mat in the making. Avoid bent-tooth combs, which the AKC says can snag and damage the coat.

## How often should you brush a Golden Retriever?

**Weekly in normal weeks, daily in heavy shedding, and before and after every bath.** That schedule comes straight from the AKC's Golden grooming guide, which quotes Talbott: "For heavy shedding, brush with a pin brush once a day to remove the dead hair." For regular shedding it says brush weekly, and brush before and after every bath. The AKC's double-coat guide adds that combing two to three times a week helps prevent mats in double coats generally.

A practical rhythm for a house pet:

- **Most weeks:** one thorough session with the pin brush, then the comb on the feathering.
- **Shedding season:** a short daily session with the pin brush or rake, then a weekly deeper rake pass.
- **Bath days:** brush first to loosen the dead undercoat, bathe, rinse well, dry fully, then brush again. The AKC notes the double coat holds shampoo, so rinse twice.

VCA Hospitals adds that dogs with heavy undercoats may benefit from baths in spring or fall during shedding, and that brushing out burrs and tangles before the bath matters because they are harder to remove once wet.

## When do Goldens shed the most?

**Goldens shed moderately all year and "blow coat" heavily once or twice a year, usually in late spring and late fall.** VCA Hospitals says retrievers often have two heavy seasonal shedding cycles per year, when much of the undercoat falls out. The AKC calls the period of heavy undercoat loss "blowing coat."

During a blow, frequent short sessions beat one long one. If your Golden loses hair in patches, scratches constantly, or has red or flaky skin, that is a reason to call your vet, not buy another brush. Sudden, patchy hair loss is not normal shedding. ## Should you shave a Golden Retriever?

**No. Shaving does not keep a Golden cooler and can damage the coat.** The AKC says shaving removes the insulating undercoat, that dogs cool mainly by panting and through their paw pads and not through their fur, and that a shaved double coat can regrow unevenly. The undercoat often grows back faster than the guard hairs and crowds them out, leaving a patchy coat that no longer repels water as well.

The AKC says shaving is generally justified only before surgery or as a last resort for severe matting that cannot be safely brushed out. For heat, the AKC suggests regular brushing to remove dead hair, cool baths, shade, water, and exercise in cooler hours. If you want a tidier look, trim only what the breed standard allows: the feet and stray hairs, with thinning shears on the feathering, per the AKC's Golden guide.

## How do you stop mats behind the ears and in the feathering?

**Mats start where hair rubs against skin or gear, and the feathering behind the ears is the classic Golden trouble spot.** PetMD lists high-friction areas to check regularly: behind the ears, under the legs and armpits, around the collar or harness, between the toes, and around the tail and hindquarters. A mat pulls on surrounding hair and skin with every movement and traps moisture and dirt against the skin.

To prevent them:

1. Comb the feathering every week, working from the tips toward the skin.
2. Check under the collar or harness each session, since friction creates tangles.
3. Dry the coat completely after swimming or a bath, because moisture speeds matting.

If you find a mat, loosen it gently with your fingers and comb. VCA Hospitals says to use clippers, never scissors, to remove mats, because the skin can be closer than it looks. PetMD recommends seeing a groomer or vet if a mat is tight to the skin, covers a large area, is near the ears, or the skin is red, raw, or smelly. ## How do you brush a senior Golden Retriever?

**Use gentler tools, lighter pressure, and shorter sessions, and treat every brushing as a body check.** Older skin is thinner and joints are stiffer. Our [senior dog grooming guide](/senior-dog-grooming/senior-dog-grooming-daily-care-guide/) covers the broader routine; here is the brush-specific part.

- **Tools:** Favor a soft pin brush and a wide-tooth comb. Skip deshedding blades and press-heavy slickers unless your vet or groomer says they are fine for your dog.
- **Position:** Let a stiff dog lie down on a comfortable surface. If your senior Golden struggles to lie down or stand, a supportive bed can make the session easier; see our [best dog beds for Golden Retrievers](/senior-dog-gear/best-dog-beds-for-golden-retrievers/).
- **Sessions:** Five calm minutes with treats beat a thorough but stressful half hour.
- **Lumps and bumps:** VCA Hospitals recommends running your hands over the coat regularly, especially under the armpits, in the groin, and behind the ears, to find unusual lumps, fleas, ticks, and sore spots. Note any new lump's size and location, and have your vet examine one that is new, growing, or changing. Do not wait to see if it goes away.
- **Pain:** If your dog flinches, pulls away, or licks a spot, mention it to your vet. Pain on touch is information.

## How to choose a brush for your Golden

**Match the tool to the job and start gentle.** Begin with a pin brush with longer, rounded-tip pins. Add one undercoat tool, either a slicker for thick areas or a rake for shedding season, and a steel comb with medium to wide teeth for the feathering. Treat a deshedding blade as optional, and size it to your dog's weight from the listing. Look for a grip you can hold comfortably, rounded pin tips, and a self-cleaning button if clogged hair annoys you. Introduce each tool with treats.

## FAQ

### What is the best brush for a Golden Retriever?

A pin brush for most weeks, plus a slicker or undercoat rake for loose undercoat, and a steel comb for the feathering. No single brush does all three jobs. The AKC's Golden guide names the pin brush and a metal comb as the core tools.

### How often should I brush my Golden Retriever?

Weekly during normal weeks, daily during heavy shedding with a pin brush, and before and after each bath. That is the guidance from a GRCA education director quoted by the AKC.

### Can I shave my Golden Retriever in summer?

No. The AKC says shaving removes the insulating undercoat, does not meaningfully cool a dog, and can cause uneven regrowth. Brush, offer shade and water, and exercise in cooler hours instead.

### How do I get mats out from behind my Golden's ears?

Work the mat loose gently with your fingers and a comb, starting at the tip. Never use scissors; VCA says clippers only. If the mat is tight, near the ear, or the skin looks sore, go to a groomer or vet.

### Is a FURminator good for Golden Retrievers?

It can pull out a lot of undercoat in a shedding season, but it is easy to overuse, and the AKC's Golden grooming guide does not mention it. If you use one, go lightly, only during heavy shedding, and never over irritated skin or mats.

### What should I use on a senior Golden with sensitive skin?

A soft pin brush and a wide-tooth comb, light pressure, and short sessions. Skip deshedding blades unless your vet or groomer approves, and check for lumps at every session.

## The bottom line

Start with a long-pin pin brush and a steel comb, add a slicker or undercoat rake for shedding season, and treat a deshedding blade as optional. Brush weekly, daily when the coat blows, and before and after baths. Check behind the ears for mats, never shave, and for an older Golden use gentler tools and shorter sessions while you check for lumps.
