Dogs don't cry about pain the way we do. They go quiet. They stop greeting you at the door. And because these changes creep in slowly, owners routinely mistake months of pain for "just getting old."

This guide is the checklist we wish every senior-dog owner had on the fridge: the subtle signs of pain in dogs, how to tell pain apart from normal aging, the difference between acute and chronic pain, how vets actually measure pain in animals that can't speak — and the one thing you must never do. For the broader mobility picture, see our [senior dog mobility guide](/senior-dog-mobility/senior-dog-mobility-guide/).

## How do I know if my senior dog is in pain?

Start with this checklist. If you recognize several of these — especially ones that are *new* — treat it as pain until your vet says otherwise:

- [ ] Stiff or slow to rise after resting; "warms out of it" with movement
- [ ] Rising using the front legs first, rocking the back end up
- [ ] Hesitates at stairs, jumps, or getting into the car
- [ ] Reluctant to cross slippery floors they used to ignore
- [ ] Limping, even intermittently, or shifting weight off a leg
- [ ] Abnormal wear on the toenails from scuffing or dragging
- [ ] Less interested in walks, play, or greeting rituals
- [ ] Panting, restlessness, or pacing when they should be settled
- [ ] Groaning or sighing when lying down or shifting position
- [ ] Withdrawing — hiding, avoiding family, leaving the room
- [ ] Irritable when touched, lifted, or groomed in certain spots
- [ ] Licking, chewing, or biting at one area repeatedly
- [ ] Appetite down, or dropping food / chewing on one side
- [ ] Whimpering, whining, or yelping when moving (late, obvious sign)
- [ ] Sleeping much more, or unable to settle comfortably
- [ ] House-soiling in a previously reliable dog (can't posture or hold it)

The pattern matters more than any single item. One off-day means little; a two-week trend of three or four of these means a vet visit. Keep a simple log for a week — what you saw, when, and what seemed to trigger it. Vets *love* this data; "she's stiff every morning for about twenty minutes, then fine" is diagnostically useful in a way "she seems sore" isn't.

## What are the subtle signs of pain in dogs?

The obvious signs — limping, yelping — are the tip of the iceberg. Most chronic pain in senior dogs shows up as **behavior**, and it's subtle precisely because dogs evolved to mask vulnerability. Yes, dogs hide pain: masking weakness is an evolved survival behavior, and it persists in domestic dogs. Assume stoicism, not comfort.

**Behavioral indicators** (the ones owners miss longest):

- **Activity drop.** The dog who gradually stops playing, greets you from the bed instead of the door, or cuts walks short. Owners adapt to this so gradually they don't notice the baseline moved.
- **Social withdrawal.** Leaving the family room, avoiding the kids' chaos, no longer soliciting petting. Pain makes dogs conserve energy and avoid jostling.
- **Restlessness.** The flip side: can't settle, circles the bed, pants at night. Restlessness plus panting in a resting dog is a classic pain signal.
- **Appetite and chewing changes.** Eating slower, dropping kibble, preferring soft food, or eating only from your hand. Mouth pain (dental disease is rampant in seniors) and nausea both do this.
- **Grooming changes.** Excessive licking of one joint or paw — dogs lick what hurts. Conversely, a dog who stops grooming a hard-to-reach area may be too stiff to manage it.
- **Personality shifts.** A sweet dog who starts growling when lifted, or a bold dog who becomes clingy and anxious. New irritability in a senior dog is pain until proven otherwise — and a new growl is communication, not disobedience. Never punish it; note what triggered it and tell your vet.

**Physiological indicators** (what the body shows):

- **Panting at rest**, especially with a tense face
- **Trembling or shaking** unrelated to cold or fear
- **A hunched or "roached" back**, tucked belly — the classic abdominal-pain posture
- **Dilated pupils, glazed or squinting eyes**, flattened ears, a tense mouth
- **Rapid, shallow breathing** or elevated heart rate at rest
- **Drooling** beyond the dog's normal

Veterinary behaviorists note that owners are excellent at spotting *change* and poor at judging *cause* — which is exactly right. Your job is to notice and document; the vet's job is to interpret.

## Where does it hurt? Reading pain by body region

The checklist tells you *that* something hurts; the location helps your vet figure out *what*. You're not diagnosing — you're giving the vet a head start.

**Mouth and teeth.** Dropping food, chewing on one side, preferring soft food, pawing at the face, bad breath, or flinching when the muzzle is touched. Dental disease is one of the most under-recognized pain sources in senior dogs — and one of the most fixable.

**Joints and limbs.** Limping, stiffness after rest, licking or chewing at one joint, reluctance on stairs, shifting weight. The classic osteoarthritis picture — and the focus of our [mobility guide](/senior-dog-mobility/senior-dog-mobility-guide/).

**Neck and spine.** Yelping when picked up or when the collar is pulled, a stiff held-high or held-low neck, reluctance to lower the head to the food bowl, hunching. Spinal pain often shows up during handling rather than walking.

**Belly.** The "prayer position" (front down, rear up), a tucked roached back, restlessness that won't resolve, stretching repeatedly, or guarding the abdomen when touched. Abdominal pain can signal anything from GI upset to emergencies like bloat — take new belly pain seriously and call promptly.

**Generalized / whole-body.** The vague signs: withdrawal, sleep changes, appetite loss, new anxiety. These point to pain *somewhere* without localizing it — which is still valuable information, because it rules out "just a mood."

Note exactly where your hands were and what movement triggered a reaction. "Yelps when I lift under the chest, fine when I lift under the belly" is the kind of detail that shortens a diagnosis.

## Acute vs. chronic pain — and why the distinction matters

Not all pain behaves the same, and the kind determines the plan.

**Acute pain** is sudden, obvious, and protective: the yelp after a bad landing, the swelling after a cut, post-surgical pain. It has a clear cause, a clear timeline, and it usually resolves as the injury heals. You will not miss acute pain — and it often needs same-day veterinary care.

**Chronic pain** is the senior dog's world. It develops gradually, persists beyond healing, and — crucially — it rewires the nervous system itself, making the dog more sensitive to pain over time (veterinary pain specialists call this maladaptive pain). Osteoarthritis is the classic example. Chronic pain rarely announces itself; it shows up as the behavioral drift described above, and owners routinely normalize it for months.

Why the distinction matters: acute pain gets short-term treatment aimed at the cause. Chronic pain gets a *long-term management plan* — layered medications, weight control, environment changes, rehab — reassessed regularly. And it's why the "wait until it's bad enough" instinct backfires: by the time chronic pain is obvious, the dog has usually been compensating for months.

## Is it pain or just old age?

This is the question that keeps owners up at night, and the framework is simpler than it feels.

**Normal aging is gradual, symmetrical, and stable.** The dog is a little slower everywhere, naps a little longer, plays a little less — and the trajectory is flat over months. They're still *themselves*: engaged, appetent, comfortable at rest.

**Pain is a change with a direction.** Something got worse over weeks: the stairs that were fine in spring are a problem by fall. There's asymmetry — one leg, one side, one spot they guard. There are bad days and better days. And there are the tells above: the panting, the withdrawal, the irritability.

One more thing worth stating plainly: [VCA Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/arthritis-in-dogs) notes that "it is now clear that simple aging is **not** a cause of OA." "Slowing down" is how owners describe arthritis, dental pain, and cognitive decline alike — and most of those are treatable. Our standing rule: **any noticeable functional change in a senior dog gets a vet visit, not an age attribution.**

One more distinction: stiffness that eases with gentle movement suggests joint pain; weakness, toe-dragging, knuckling, or a wobbly hind end suggests neurological causes. Film the gait if you can; video beats verbal description in the exam room every time.

## What should I do if I think my dog is in pain?

Don't wait for a "bad enough" threshold — chronic pain managed early responds better than chronic pain managed late. Here's the sequence:

**1. Call your vet.** Describe the specific changes and their timeline — your week of notes pays off here. Mention any video you've taken.

**2. Make them comfortable tonight.** A warm (not hot), supportive resting spot. Non-slip footing to the water bowl and the door. Shorten tomorrow's walk. Keep the routine calm and predictable — pain and anxiety amplify each other.

**3. Do not medicate on your own.** This deserves its own box:

> **⚠️ Never give human painkillers to your dog.** Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) is, per the [MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/toxicology/toxicoses-from-human-analgesics/toxicoses-from-human-analgesics-in-animals), no longer recommended in dogs at all — it has a narrow margin of safety, and GI ulceration, hemorrhage, and kidney damage are the most commonly reported toxic effects. Acetaminophen (Tylenol) can destroy a dog's red blood cells and damage the liver. Aspirin is also dangerous without veterinary direction, carrying real risks of GI ulceration. There are no safe "just this once" human painkillers for dogs. If your dog is suffering tonight, call your vet or an emergency clinic — they can advise or prescribe something actually safe.

**4. Watch for escalation.** Pain that suddenly worsens, a dog who can't rise at all, continuous vocalizing, or pain paired with vomiting, collapse, labored breathing, or a swollen belly — that's urgent, not routine.

After the diagnosis, the management plan usually combines veterinary treatment (NSAIDs, other prescribed pain control, rehab) with the home changes in our [mobility guide](/senior-dog-mobility/senior-dog-mobility-guide/): weight, flooring, ramps, bedding. Pain control and environment work together — medication without a non-slip floor is half a plan.

## How do vets assess pain in dogs?

Knowing what happens in the exam room makes the visit less mysterious — and your observations more useful. Pain assessment in dogs is a structured clinical process, not guesswork.

**History first.** Your notes — what changed, when, what helps, what worsens it — are the most valuable diagnostic tool in the room. Bring the video.

**Hands-on exam.** The vet systematically flexes and extends joints, palpates the spine and muscles, and watches the gait — looking for pain responses, reduced range of motion, muscle asymmetry, and neurological signs.

**Validated pain scales.** Because dogs can't self-report, veterinary medicine built structured questionnaires around exactly the behavioral changes described in this guide. The best-known is the **Canine Brief Pain Inventory (CBPI)**, an owner-completed questionnaire that scores two factors: pain *severity* (worst, least, average, and current pain) and pain *interference* with function (general activity, enjoyment of life, ability to rise, walk, run, and climb). It isn't just a form — it was validated in a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 70 dogs with osteoarthritis, where it successfully detected improvement in dogs treated with the NSAID carprofen versus placebo (Brown et al., *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*, 2008; [abstract](https://www.medscape.com/medline/abstract/19180716)). A follow-up study comparing CBPI scores against force-plate gait analysis found owners were detecting real functional change — including behavioral dimensions beyond simple lameness (Brown et al., *Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine*, 2013). The Liverpool Osteoarthritis in Dogs (LOAD) questionnaire is another validated owner tool used the same way.

**Imaging and labs.** X-rays confirm arthritis and rule out fractures, tumors, or disc disease; bloodwork checks organ function (important before starting long-term pain medication) and screens for the metabolic conditions that mimic or compound pain.

And remember: "she seems fine at the vet" doesn't overrule two weeks of careful notes at home. Adrenaline masks pain beautifully. Trust your observations — you see the unguarded dog.

## How can I monitor my dog's pain at home?

Vets consistently say the same thing: the owner who brings notes gets a better diagnosis than the owner who brings vibes. A simple weekly pain log takes two minutes and turns "she seems sore" into actionable data.

**The one-line daily log.** Each evening, jot one line: date, a 1–5 comfort score (1 = clearly painful, 5 = normal self), and any notable signs. Example: "Tue — 3/5 — stiff 15 min in morning, skipped stairs, fine by afternoon walk." After two weeks, patterns emerge that no single observation reveals: worse in mornings (classic arthritis), worse after long walks (overexertion), worse in damp weather (commonly reported), or steadily declining (time to reassess the plan).

**Film the gait monthly.** Thirty seconds of your dog walking toward you, away, and from the side, on a non-slip surface. Date the clips. Comparing month to month reveals gradual decline your eyes adapt to day by day — the single most useful thing you can show a vet.

**Track the "joy metrics."** Validated pain scales are built on behavior, so track behaviors: Does the dog still greet you at the door? Initiate play? Settle comfortably, or circle endlessly? Eat with normal enthusiasm? These are your early-warning system and, later, your measure of whether treatment is working.

**Medication check-ins.** If your vet prescribes pain medication, the log becomes a treatment diary: note start dates, dose changes (as directed by the vet — never on your own), appetite, energy, and any vomiting, diarrhea, or appetite loss. Report GI side effects promptly; don't just stop the drug without calling. Improvement usually shows as returning *behaviors* — the greeting, the walk enthusiasm — within days to a couple of weeks.

**When the log says "call."** A comfort score stuck at 1–2 for several days, a new sign appearing (vocalizing, not eating, can't rise), or side effects from medication — all warrant a call, not another week of logging. The log informs the vet; it never replaces them.

## When is pain an emergency?

Most chronic pain is a "book this week" situation. Call now — emergency clinic if after hours — for sudden severe pain with no clear cause, a dog who suddenly can't rise or walk, continuous crying or whimpering that doesn't resolve, pain paired with vomiting, collapse, labored breathing, or a swollen hard belly (possible bloat, which is life-threatening), or any known trauma. When in doubt, call and describe it; clinics triage by phone, and they'd rather hear from you early.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do dogs hide pain?
Yes — masking pain is an evolved survival behavior, and it persists in domestic dogs. That's why chronic pain shows up as behavior change (withdrawal, restlessness, irritability) rather than crying. Assume stoicism, not comfort.

### Why is my senior dog panting and restless at night?
Pain and canine cognitive dysfunction are the two most common causes of nighttime restlessness in seniors — sometimes both. Track when it happens and mention it at the next vet visit; see also our [behavior changes guide](/senior-dog-behavior/senior-dog-behavior-changes-guide/).

### Can I give my dog aspirin for pain?
Not without explicit veterinary direction. Aspirin carries real risks of GI ulceration and interacts badly with other medications; ibuprofen and acetaminophen are outright toxic to dogs. Call your vet for safe options instead.

### Is limping always an emergency?
Not always — a mild, intermittent limp that resolves is a "book this week" situation. Sudden non-weight-bearing lameness, a hot swollen joint, or limping after trauma is urgent. When in doubt, call and describe it; clinics triage by phone.

### My dog yelps when I pick them up. What does that mean?
Usually spinal or abdominal pain, or a painful spot being compressed. Note exactly where your hands were and what movement triggered it, and get a vet appointment promptly — this one deserves hands-on assessment, not watchful waiting.

### My senior dog growled when I touched them — is that a behavior problem?
Treat a new growl as communication first and a behavior problem never — in a senior dog, it's most often pain. Note what you touched and how, don't punish the growl (it's the warning system working), and bring the observation to your vet.
