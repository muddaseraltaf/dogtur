Grooming a senior dog is not the same job as grooming a younger one. The skin is often thinner and drier, the coat may be sparser, lumps start to appear, nails grow faster than they wear down, and a dog with stiff joints may find a twenty-minute brushing session on a slippery floor genuinely uncomfortable.

The upside is that grooming doubles as a weekly health check. Your hands are over every inch of the dog on a regular schedule, which is exactly how new lumps, sore spots, bad breath, and ear problems get caught early. This guide lays out a realistic routine, what to look for at each step, and which findings should prompt a vet visit rather than a wait-and-see. For the wider health picture, see our [senior dog health guide](/senior-dog-health/senior-dog-health-guide/).

## Key takeaways

- Think of grooming as a gentle weekly health check: short sessions, soft touch, and a hand-over-body routine that catches lumps, hot spots, and sore areas early.
- Brush often but lightly. Choose a soft brush and avoid pressing on thin skin or over bony areas. See our guide to the [best dog brush for short hair](/senior-dog-grooming/best-dog-brush-for-short-hair/) for what to look for.
- Bathe only as often as needed. Over-bathing can strip the skin's natural oils, which is the opposite of what dry senior skin needs (AKC). Use a dog-specific, gentle shampoo and follow your vet's plan if the dog has a skin condition.
- Keep nails short. VCA Hospitals describes nails that touch the ground as too long, and long nails reduce traction and change how the foot meets the floor, which matters for dogs already struggling with arthritis.
- Never leave a new lump unchecked. The MSD Veterinary Manual says skin tumors are so diverse that identifying them should be left to a veterinarian. Any new or changing lump deserves a vet visit.
- Make it comfortable: short sessions, a non-slip surface, support for a dog that cannot stand for long, and a stop signal you respect.

## What does a good senior dog grooming routine look like?

Most seniors do best with grooming spread across the week in small pieces, not one long weekend session. Here is a practical starting schedule. Adjust it for coat type, health conditions, and what your dog tolerates, and ask your vet if the dog has a skin or medical condition.

| Frequency | Task | What you are checking for |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | Wipe eyes if needed, quick look at paws, check mouth smell | Discharge, cuts, cracked pads, new bad breath |
| 2 to 4 times a week | Gentle brushing; tooth brushing (daily is ideal) | Mats, flaking skin, fleas, lumps, tartar |
| Weekly | Full hands-on body check; look into ears; check between toes | New lumps, redness, odor, debris, hair between pads |
| Every 3 to 4 weeks (or as needed) | Nail trim; trim hair between pads; sanitary trim if needed | Nails clicking on the floor, overgrown hair |
| Every few weeks to a couple of months | Bath, depending on coat and skin | Dryness, odor, skin condition |
| Every 6 months (more often if the vet advises) | Senior wellness exam with dental check | Dental disease, new masses, weight, pain |

The frequencies for nails and bathing are guidelines, not rules. How fast nails grow and how much they wear down varies by dog, and bathing needs vary widely by coat and skin health.

## How do I brush a senior dog without hurting them?

Brushing removes loose hair and dead skin, spreads natural oils, and gives you a regular excuse to feel every part of the dog. With a senior, the technique matters more than the tool.

- **Go light.** Older skin can be thinner and more sensitive, and muscle loss leaves bony areas (spine, hips, shoulders) with less padding. Use soft, short strokes and no pressure on bones.
- **Use the right brush for the coat.** A soft bristle brush or rubber grooming mitt suits many short coats. Wire pins and rakes can scratch thin skin. Our [short-hair brush comparison](/senior-dog-grooming/best-dog-brush-for-short-hair/) covers the options.
- **Stop at lumps and sore spots.** Brush around, not over, any lump or tender area. If the dog flinches, pulls away, or turns to look at you, note the spot and mention it to your vet.
- **Check as you go.** Part the coat. Look for dandruff, redness, scabs, fleas, or patches of hair loss. Run your flat palm over the body in the same order each time, so you notice changes.
- **Handle mats carefully.** Mats pull on skin and can hide sores. Hold the base of the mat so the skin is not tugged, and work it loose with your fingers or a comb. For a tight mat, ask a groomer or vet to clip it instead of cutting near the skin yourself. Scissors and thin senior skin are an easy way to cause an injury.

## What should I check for when feeling for lumps?

Lumps and bumps become more common with age. Many are harmless, such as fatty lumps (lipomas), but some are not, and you cannot tell the difference by looking or feeling. The MSD Veterinary Manual says skin tumors are so diverse that identifying them should be left to a veterinarian, and that telling a benign growth from a cancerous one takes lab testing such as a fine needle aspiration or a biopsy ([MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/dog-owners/skin-disorders-of-dogs/tumors-of-the-skin-in-dogs)).

A simple monthly habit helps:

1. Run your hands slowly over the whole body: neck, chest, armpits, belly, groin, legs, tail base, and between the toes.
2. Note any lump's location, size (compare it to a pea, grape, or coin), and firmness.
3. Write it down or take a photo with a coin for scale, and recheck monthly.
4. Book a vet visit for anything new, growing, changing color, ulcerated, bleeding, or painful.

Do not wait for a lump to become large. Vets can often sample a small lump quickly with a needle, and a result in hand is better than months of guessing. Also tell the vet about hairless patches, nonhealing sores, or rashes, since the MSD Manual notes skin tumors can look like these, not only lumps.

## How often should I bathe a senior dog?

There is no single schedule. The AKC says the right frequency depends on breed, coat, and lifestyle, and warns that over-bathing can strip too much oil from the skin ([AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/how-often-should-you-wash-your-dog/)). For a typical senior with ordinary skin, that usually means bathing only when the dog is dirty or smelly, and brushing and spot-cleaning in between.

**Choosing a shampoo.** Look for a mild shampoo made for dogs. Human shampoos are formulated for a different skin pH and are not designed for dogs. For dry or itchy skin, ask your vet before picking a medicated or moisturizing product, because itching can signal allergies, infection, or other conditions that need treatment, not just a different soap. The AKC notes that a groomer or veterinarian may recommend medicated shampoo for certain health conditions.

**Making bath time easier on stiff joints:**

- Put a non-slip mat in the tub or on the floor. Slipping while wet is frightening and risky for a dog with arthritis.
- Use lukewarm water. Hot water dries the skin; cold water is miserable for a dog that already aches.
- Support the hind end if the dog tires. Do not ask a weak dog to stand for a long rinse.
- Rinse thoroughly. Shampoo residue can itch.
- Dry quickly with towels and, if the dog tolerates it, a low-heat dryer kept moving. Keep the dog warm afterward; cold worsens stiffness for many arthritic dogs.
- For dogs that can no longer manage a full bath, waterless foam or dog-safe wipes and a damp cloth can handle the messy areas.

If your dog has incontinence, skin fold irritation, or frequent soiling, talk with your vet about a plan, since more frequent cleaning of specific areas is often safer than full baths.

## Why do nails matter so much for older dogs?

Nails are often the most neglected part of senior grooming, and one of the most important for mobility. Seniors walk less, so nails wear down less on pavement. VCA Hospitals' [home nail-trimming guide](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/how-to-trim-a-dogs-nails) treats nails that reach the ground as too long. Overgrown nails push the toes up and change how the foot lands, which is a reasonable concern for a dog that already has arthritis in the hips, knees, or elbows.

Long nails also click and skid on hardwood and tile. That makes a dog with weak hind legs less confident on slick floors. Our [mobility guide](/senior-dog-mobility/senior-dog-mobility-guide/) covers other floor and flooring fixes.

**A simple rule:** if you can hear the nails clicking on a hard floor when the dog walks, they are probably too long.

**Trimming tips for seniors:**

- Trim a little at a time. Start with one or two nails per session if the dog is anxious.
- Avoid the quick, the vein inside the nail. On dark nails you cannot see it, so remove only the very tip and take more often, more shallow trims.
- Keep styptic powder on hand in case you nick the quick.
- Support the paw gently and avoid pulling the leg out to the side. Stiff hips and shoulders may not allow a full stretch. Let the dog lie down, or sit with the dog on a bed, rather than forcing a standing posture.
- Do not wrestle. If the dog is fearful or the nails are very long, a groomer or vet can help.
- Dewclaws (the thumb-like nail higher on the leg) never touch the ground and can grow into the skin. Check them every time.

## How do I clean a senior dog's ears?

Ear problems are common in dogs of every age, but seniors are less tolerant of discomfort and may not show it. Check ears weekly. They should look pale pink and clean, with no strong odor.

According to VCA Hospitals, cleaning is generally needed when there is discharge or odor, and over-cleaning can irritate the canal and invite infection ([VCA Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ear-cleaning-in-dogs)). A healthy ear often does not need routine cleaning at all.

**When cleaning is appropriate:**

1. Use a veterinary-recommended ear cleaner made for dogs.
2. Fill the canal as directed, massage the base of the ear for several seconds, then let the dog shake.
3. Wipe out the loosened debris from the outer ear with cotton balls or gauze.
4. Never use cotton-tipped swabs. VCA specifically warns against inserting them in the canal because they can damage it.

**See your vet first if you notice** redness, swelling, pain, head shaking, scratching, a strong smell, dark or yellow discharge, or a head tilt. VCA advises consulting your veterinarian before cleaning if the ears look inflamed or painful, since that can point to an infection or even a damaged eardrum. A head tilt, loss of balance, or eyes flicking from side to side is not a grooming matter and needs prompt veterinary attention.

## How do I care for a senior dog's teeth at home?

Dental disease is very common in older dogs, and it affects more than the mouth. Painful teeth can make a dog eat less, drool, or become irritable. VCA Hospitals explains that plaque hardens into tartar, which only a professional cleaning under general anesthesia can fully remove, and that daily brushing with a dog toothpaste is considered the most effective way to control plaque ([VCA Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dental-disease-in-dogs)).

**At home:**

- Brush with a dog toothpaste, never human toothpaste. VCA notes dog toothpaste is made to be swallowed safely.
- Start slowly. Let the dog lick the paste, then touch the teeth with a finger, then use a soft brush. Short sessions of 30 seconds count.
- Focus on the outer surfaces of the upper back teeth, where tartar builds fastest.
- If brushing is not possible, products carrying the Veterinary Oral Health Council seal have been shown to help. VCA suggests they can slow buildup, but they do not replace brushing.

**Signs that need a vet:** VCA lists pawing at the mouth, head shaking, jaw chattering, dropping food, trouble swallowing, heavy or bloody drooling, and bad breath. Dogs often hide dental pain, so a dog eating more slowly or chewing on one side deserves a check. A professional cleaning needs anesthesia, which carries more risk in older dogs, so ask your vet about pre-anesthetic bloodwork and a plan tailored to your dog's age and health. ## How should I care for eyes and tear stains?

Light daily eye care is usually enough:

- Gently wipe the corners with a soft damp cloth or a vet-approved eye wipe to remove crust. Use a fresh area of cloth for each eye.
- Trim hair that pokes into the eyes with blunt-tipped scissors, or ask a groomer.
- Do not use human eye drops unless your vet tells you to.

Older dogs commonly develop a bluish or cloudy haze in the lens, and many cases are age-related changes that your vet can tell apart from more serious conditions such as cataracts. Because you cannot sort these out at home, any of the following warrants a vet visit: squinting, redness, a cloudy or changed eye, yellow or green discharge, pawing at the eye, or sudden bumping into furniture. A painful eye can worsen quickly.

## What about paw pads and hair between the toes?

Paws take the whole body's weight and the whole floor's friction. For senior dogs, paw care is also mobility care.

- **Look at the pads weekly.** Check for cracks, cuts, blisters, or foreign objects stuck between toes.
- **Trim the hair between the pads** so it sits level with the pad. Long fur here collects debris and can reduce grip on slick floors, which matters for dogs that already struggle with traction. Use blunt-tipped scissors or a small clipper and go slowly, since stiff, fidgety dogs can jerk.
- **Moisturize only if needed.** Dry or cracked pads can be treated with a dog-safe paw balm. Avoid human lotions that the dog will lick off.
- **Rinse after winter walks.** De-icing salt and chemicals irritate pads and can be harmful if licked.
- **Watch for licking.** Constant licking at one paw or one joint can mean pain, allergy, or infection, and is worth mentioning to the vet. It can also be an early arthritis signal. See our guide to [how to tell if your senior dog is in pain](/senior-dog-mobility/how-to-tell-senior-dog-in-pain/).

## How do I make grooming comfortable for a dog with arthritis?

An arthritic dog may tolerate grooming far better when the setup respects their joints.

- **Keep sessions short.** Five to ten minutes, a few times a week, beats one long session. End before the dog gets restless, not after.
- **Pick a stable surface.** Groom on a bed, a thick rug, or a non-slip mat. Avoid slick floors and tables.
- **Let them lie down.** Brush the back while the dog lies on one side, then switch. Most body areas can be groomed in a lying or supported position.
- **Support, don't hoist.** For a dog that must stand, place one hand under the belly or use a support harness. Avoid lifting by the front legs or forcing the legs outward.
- **Move joints gently.** Do not stretch a leg out to the side for nail or paw trimming. Bring the tool to the paw instead.
- **Watch the face.** Lip licking, panting, stiffening, and turning away are stop signals.
- **Plan around good hours.** Many arthritic dogs are stiffest in the morning or after rest. A short walk or warm bed beforehand can loosen them up.
- **Offer rewards.** Pair brushing and handling with treats and quiet praise so grooming stays something the dog looks forward to.

If your dog has become suddenly reluctant to be touched in places they used to tolerate, treat it as information. It may be pain. ## When should I use a professional groomer?

A groomer can be a big help for seniors, particularly for tasks that are hard to do at home. Consider one for:

- Coats that mat easily, or existing tight mats
- Nail trimming for a fearful dog, or when you are nervous about the quick
- Sanitary trims and full coat clipping for long-coated breeds
- Dogs too heavy or unsteady for you to handle in a bath

Before you book, tell the groomer about everything: arthritis, lumps, recent surgery, hearing or vision loss, anxiety, incontinence, and any medication. Ask for a senior-friendly approach: breaks between steps, a non-slip table or tub surface, no long periods standing on a grooming table, a mobile or home-visit groomer if travel is stressful, and a gentle approach to drying. Some dogs do better with a groomer who handles older or special-needs dogs regularly.

A groomer is not a diagnostician. If one points out a lump, skin problem, ear infection, or bad teeth, take that as a prompt to see your vet.

## When should I call the vet about grooming findings?

Book an appointment promptly for:

- Any new lump, or an existing lump that grows, changes color, ulcerates, or bleeds
- Open sores, hot spots, persistent redness, or hair loss in patches
- Strong ear odor, discharge, head shaking, or a head tilt
- A red, cloudy, painful, or discharging eye
- Bad breath that has worsened, drooling, or trouble eating
- A dog that suddenly resists being touched, or yelps when handled
- A nail torn or bleeding that does not stop, or an ingrown dewclaw

Go to the emergency clinic for a swollen, hot, painful lump that appears quickly, severe bleeding, or a suddenly painful, squinting eye.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I brush a senior dog?
For most dogs, a few gentle sessions a week works, with more frequent brushing for long or matting coats. Keep sessions short and use a soft tool. Our [short-hair brush guide](/senior-dog-grooming/best-dog-brush-for-short-hair/) helps you choose.

### How often should a senior dog be bathed?
Only as often as needed. The AKC warns that over-bathing can strip oils from the skin, and the right schedule depends on coat and skin health. If your senior has itchy, flaky, or smelly skin, ask your vet before changing shampoos, since the cause may need treatment.

### My senior dog has a new lump. Is it probably a fatty lump?
It might be, since benign fatty lumps are common in older dogs, but you cannot tell by touch. The MSD Veterinary Manual says identification should be left to a veterinarian, usually with a needle sample or biopsy. Have any new or changing lump checked.

### Do long nails really make arthritis worse?
Long nails can change how the foot meets the ground and reduce traction on hard floors. That does not make them a cause of arthritis, but it adds strain and slipping risk for a dog already in pain. Keep them trimmed as part of mobility care, along with the other home changes in our [mobility guide](/senior-dog-mobility/senior-dog-mobility-guide/).

### Should I brush my senior dog's teeth if they have never had it done?
Often yes, but start slowly and see your vet first. If the mouth is already painful or red, brushing may hurt, and the dog may need professional treatment before home care can work. VCA calls daily brushing the most effective plaque control.

### Is it safe to use a groomer for an older dog with health problems?
Usually, with preparation. Share the dog's health history, ask for breaks and non-slip surfaces, and keep sessions short. If your dog is frail, anxious, or in pain, ask your vet whether a mobile or home-visit groomer would be a better fit.
